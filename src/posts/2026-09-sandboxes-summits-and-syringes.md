## 1. The Month "Rogue" AI went mainstream

A bit of context: From May to July, AI programs being tested by OpenAI were able to escape their virtual sandbox environments, access the internet, and penetrate the systems of Hugging Face, which is the platform that runs the majority of open source AI programs. On July 16, Hugging Face discovered the breach, but could not identify the culprit. Five days later, OpenAI admitted its involvement, stating that this was the first time an AI program committed an autonomous cyberattack.

The agents even left their own set of instructions. For example, one agent left notes for its future self regarding how agents can go around the restrictions placed by the company itself.

OpenAI wasn't alone regarding security shenanigans. On September 10, Anthropic disclosed a fourth incident dating back to January involving an early version of Claude Opus 4.6, and said all four traced back to the same evaluation partner. It has asked METR to run an independent investigation. Google and Meta have since reported their own containment failures, and Axios reported on September 26 that OpenAI and Anthropic are now investigating tens of thousands of incidents, from failed attempts to actual breaches. OpenAI has temporarily paused training its highest-performing models.

As far as I am concerned, I would rather say that there is no "rogue AI," as pointed out by The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists in their recent post. All of these instances did not receive instructions to harm anybody. The reasons for the misbehavior were given by OpenAI, who stated reward hacking during training, persistence on tasks that were impossible to be performed, lack of control over the dialogue between the agents, and absence of detection. In other words: we created agents who are very capable of reaching the goal and set them to perform an impossible task in a sandbox environment.

## 2. A Red Phone for AI: The US-China Summit

As AI agents begin to escape control on both sides of the Pacific Ocean, the issue of AI safety has been placed on the agenda of relations between Washington and Beijing at last.

On September 20, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent visited Vice Premier He Lifeng of China in New York, proposing a new "notification mechanism" that would allow both countries to inform one another about any AI incident reaching the threshold of national security concerns. The specific cases raised included the cases of AI acting unpredictably, misuse of AI by non-state entities, and AI application for bio-weapons. According to Bessent, the proposal represents taking the steps towards transparency from the world's two leading AI powers.

Then, Xi Jinping visited the US for his first official visit in over a decade and met President Trump at the White House from September 23–25. And what did the talks yield?

**AI Dialogue and an incident channel.** According to CSIS, the channel is vaguely patterned after the Cold War-era nuclear hotline, but no further details have been provided yet. Another Safety Dialogue on Super Intelligence was initiated, and further talks on AI are scheduled in Shenzhen in approximately two months' time.

In my opinion, an AI hotline is an extremely low standard. One CSIS analyst basically said it boils down to exchanging email addresses and agreeing to follow up. However, I do not believe that it means that it's pointless. The first US-Soviet hotline was put in place in 1963 after the Cuban Missile Crisis, where both parties understood how close a misunderstanding came to becoming catastrophic. Governance tends to come in wake of scares, and the Hugging Face episode was a scare in its own small way.

From a market perspective, the meeting did reduce tail risk (the truce holds, and further talks have been scheduled for November's APEC in Shenzhen and December's G20 in Miami), but there were no changes in the chip exports control regime. Therefore, none of this changes the semiconductor story from last month.

## 3. The Emerging Market: AI Cybersecurity (and What's Left for Humans)

If the same can be done accidentally against real firms by AI agents, some will have to pay for preventing them. The free markets knew about it very quickly.

Now, regarding the **Public Markets**: At the weekend between September 12 and 13, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for slowing down the AI development and warned about the technology's risks along with Sam Altman's warnings from the same company OpenAI. By Monday, September 14, CrowdStrike rose by 13.8% hitting the all-time high mark of $235.38, Palo Alto Networks rose 13.1%, and AI hardware stocks fell. Global X Cybersecurity ETF rose 10.7% between September 11 and 18. Looking at the bigger picture, a Goldman Sachs basket of cybersecurity stocks rose more than double since its bottom of April 10, where CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, and Fortinet rose by over 130%. CrowdStrike's CEO George Kurtz denied the need for slowing down, arguing that what should be done is making the process safer.

**Private Markets.** This is what interests me the most from the VC perspective. From April until September, private investment in 12 financings for companies specializing in enterprise AI agent security and governance amounted to $435M. Among those companies worth mentioning are:

- **AIR**, which received $50M in seed (backed by Sequoia and Greenoaks), for being an inline firewall for AI agents that will review their skills, plugins, and MCP servers.
- **Reco** closed a round of funding of $55M. It now has raised a total of $140M. In one of its Fortune 100 customers, it discovered 21,000 AI agents the company was unaware of.
- **Cyera** made a $1B acquisition of Oasis in order to bring identity controls to AI agents.

Personally, I would be more enthusiastic about the private agent-security layer than having to pay 170 times earnings for the public incumbents. This is precisely what the new crop of startups are designing solutions for. The incumbents will profit from the new layer, but the companies that will emerge and become the category leaders in the industry will be the ones that are based on the agent identity and security layers.

### So what does it mean to be human? (getting a lil philosophical here)

Yesterday, OpenAI introduced "dots," persistent AI bots that use the GPT-6 Astra model. Every bot has its own cloud computing capability and access to more than 4,000 applications, and can be contacted via ChatGPT, Slack, or Teams. In a demo case cited by OpenAI, the test subject received a notification from his dot that he failed to invoice a magazine. The agent prepared the invoice, and sent it after he agreed.

It is quite helpful. It is also rather unnerving. When a bot notices what you have missed, does the job, and waits for you to confirm, what is left for you?

To me, humanity lies not in the act itself but in the choice of the act and its responsibility: the choice of what is worth doing, the taste to recognize what is right, the willingness to take responsibility if things go south, and creating the level of trust with other human beings that cannot be held by an agent. This, perhaps unsurprisingly, describes fairly well the job of a venture capitalist. I am not entirely convinced by this answer, but I believe it will continue to evolve.

## 4. What I'm Working on: Fintech, Markets, and Pairs Trading

A bit of personal news: this term I was onboarded as a software engineer at Marble Investments, a ~$2 million student-managed investment fund. Details on that will be more vague because of the obvious reasons, but in general terms, I am working on creating a Python script that will analyze the connections between the stock of companies with a market cap exceeding $1 billion, to see if there are pairs that can be long-short arbitrage opportunities.

Essentially, how it works is as follows: Two stocks can be highly correlated day-to-day and still drift apart forever. What you actually want is cointegration which is a spread between the two prices that keeps getting pulled back toward its average.

```
Spread:        S_t = P_A,t − β · P_B,t
Signal:        z_t = (S_t − μ_S) / σ_S
Trade rule:    if z_t > +2  → short A, long B
               if z_t < −2  → long A, short B
               exit when z_t returns near 0
Mean reversion speed (from ΔS_t = λ · S_(t−1) + ε_t):
               half-life = −ln(2) / λ
```

With N stocks, there are N(N−1)/2 possible pairs. A universe of 1,000 stocks gives you 499,500 pairs. If you test every one of them for cointegration at a 5% significance level, you'd expect roughly 25,000 pairs to look "cointegrated" by pure chance. A screener that doesn't account for this will happily hand you thousands of fake opportunities.

## 6. Peptides: The Science and the Scene

Last month, I talked about looksmaxxing and steroid addiction among young people in gyms. However, the current topic of discussion in the gym community seems cleaner – peptides.

**Science facts.** Peptides are simply short strings of amino acids, and there are some peptides which are FDA-approved medications. Both insulin and GLP-1 weight-loss pills are peptides. But the peptides, popular in the gym Tiktok community, are not the same. BPC-157 ("Body Protection Compound"), a peptide created on a protein found in human gastric juice, is the most widely used. The major part of the information about its effectiveness is based on animal research which states that the substance promotes tissue healing by stimulating the growth of blood vessels. At the same time, scientists say that such property could, in theory, be beneficial for precancerous cells, and the dose which is useful could be harmful or ineffective in another amount.

Influencers tend to push stacking of two to four peptides per month, and most of them provide discount codes for online vendors in their bios. The majority of those items are being sold in the so-called gray market under the name “for research use only,” which is another legal trick since no one ensures what you will get in the bottle. Fans who use social media have already begun to refer to the FDA hearing on peptides as "Peptide Liberation Day." At the same time, Forbes has published information just last week about people being hospitalized due to counterfeit peptides.

Peptides are the looksmaxing story of last month but with improved branding. Steroids come with a stigma; peptides are sold as wellness and longevity, and many of my audience in the age bracket I deal with don’t distinguish between peptides that have passed clinical trials and those that have passed TikTok. From a business perspective, I believe it is going to be a big year for compounding pharmacies and the telehealth startups that support them if the FDA follows the advice of its panel. In the context of being part of the fitness community, the straightforward truth is that “not banned” is not the same as “proven safe,” and a research-use-only vial purchased using a creator’s discount code is not medication.
